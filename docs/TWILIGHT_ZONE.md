# The twilight zone at 200 m: the animals, their behaviour, and a field plan

This is the biology behind the vehicle's behaviour: who lives at 100–250 m in the offshore Gulf of
Mexico, where they are hour by hour, how they perceive and react to a vehicle, and what that means
for when to dive, where to go, how to search, and how to approach and film each kind of animal.
The machine-learning side comes after this is settled. docs/SEARCH.md describes what the software
does today; §9 lists what this plan asks of it.

## 1. The setting at 200 m

- **Depth zones.** 200 m is the top of the **mesopelagic** or "twilight zone", about 200–1000 m.
  Too little light reaches it for photosynthesis, but by day there is enough for animals to see
  [L: Sutton 2013].
- **Light by day.** The open Gulf is clear blue water. Blue light (~470–490 nm) penetrates
  deepest; red is gone within the first tens of metres.
  - At 200 m by day the light is roughly 0.1–1% of the surface value [E: from attenuation of
    about 0.02–0.04 per metre in clear ocean water].
  - That is plenty for animal eyes adapted to dim blue light, and very little for a small camera.
  - Looking up, animals see each other as silhouettes against the brighter water above.
    Looking sideways, the scene is much darker.
- **Light at night.** Essentially nothing but moonlight traces near the surface and
  bioluminescence (§6).
- **Temperature.** 200 m is within or below the thermocline. Expect roughly 15–20 °C, warmer
  inside Loop Current water and warm eddies [E: check a CTD profile for the site and date].
- **Pressure.** About 21 bar at 200 m. Rate the hull, window and connectors with a margin (300 m).
- **Currents.** The Loop Current and its eddies are strong near the surface and still
  substantial at 200 m [L]. The vehicle drifts with the water, so it surfaces kilometres from
  where it dived. Plan recovery around that (§7.8).
- **Oxygen.** The Gulf's oxygen minimum lies deeper, a few hundred metres down [L]. It does not
  limit animals at 200 m.
- **Water depth.** The oceanic twilight-zone community lives over deep water. Dive beyond the
  shelf break, where the bottom is well below 500 m [E].

## 2. Who lives there

The Gulf's deep-pelagic fauna is diverse: hundreds of fish species, dozens of cephalopods,
crustaceans and gelatinous animals. The DEEPEND consortium sampled it from 2015–2020 with
depth-stratified nets and acoustics [L: Sutton et al. 2020; Milligan & Sutton 2020]. Earlier
work sampled the eastern Gulf [L: Hopkins & Lancraft 1984; Gartner et al. 1987; Hopkins et al.
1996]. The groups a camera at 100–250 m will meet:

| group | size | by day | at night | on camera, and how it reacts |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Lanternfishes** (Myctophidae; about fifty Gulf species, e.g. *Ceratoscopelus*, *Lepidophanes*, *Diaphus*, *Notolychnus*) | 2–15 cm | 200–1000 m [V] | 0–200 m [L]; part of the layer rises above 100 m at dusk [V] | The dominant migrating fishes. Big eyes, rows of photophores. Seen from submersibles hanging motionless in the layer [L: Barham 1966]. Strong avoidance of nets and vehicles [L: Kaartvedt et al. 2012]. Quick burst escapes. |
| **Bristlemouths** (*Cyclothone*) | 2–7 cm | mostly 400–1000+ m, by species | mostly the same: non-migrating | Probably the most abundant vertebrates on Earth [L], but few at 200 m. |
| **Hatchetfishes** (Sternoptychidae, e.g. *Argyropelecus*) | 2–8 cm | about 300–600 m | somewhat shallower; weak or partial migrants [L] | Silvery, upward-looking tubular eyes, belly photophores. Hovers: a good subject when met. |
| **Lightfishes** (*Vinciguerria*) | 3–6 cm | mesopelagic | migrate upward [L] | Small, silvery, photophores. |
| **Dragonfishes** (Stomiidae: viperfish *Chauliodus*, *Stomias*, *Malacosteus*, *Idiacanthus*) | 5–40 cm | 300–1000 m | some rise to 100–200 m [L] | Ambush predators, often motionless, with luminous chin barbels. *Malacosteus* and relatives make and see far-red light, the exception to "fish can't see red" [L: Widder et al. 1984; Douglas et al. 1998]. |
| **Squids** (*Abralia*, *Abraliopsis*, *Pyroteuthis*; glass squids Cranchiidae; juvenile ommastrephids) | 2–20 cm | mesopelagic | many migrate upward [L: Judkins et al. 2017] | Sharp-eyed, fast; jet and ink. Belly photophores for counter-illumination. Some approach a quiet vehicle [E]. Glass squids hang transparent and still. |
| **Krill** (Euphausiidae: *Euphausia*, *Thysanopoda*, *Nematoscelis*, *Stylocheiron*) | 1–4 cm | 300–600 m | upper 100–200 m [L: Frank et al. 2020] | Strong migrants that often swarm. Tail-flip escape when the water around them moves. |
| **Shrimps** (Sergestidae, Oplophoridae e.g. *Acanthephyra*, *Janicella*, *Systellaspis*) | 2–10 cm | 300–800 m | many rise to 50–200 m [L: Burdett et al. 2017] | Deep red, which is invisible in blue light. Some spew clouds of luminous fluid when attacked. |
| **Siphonophores** (calycophorans like diphyids and abylids; physonects like *Nanomia*, *Agalma*, *Forskalia*) | cm to metres | all depths | some migrate | Colonies that fish with curtains of tentacles, some with glowing or red lures [L: Purcell 1980; Haddock et al. 2005]. Weak swimmers that do not flee. Fragile. |
| **Medusae and ctenophores** (narcomedusae *Solmissus*, *Aegina*; lobate ctenophores *Ocyropsis*, *Bolinopsis*; *Beroe*) | 1–20 cm | all depths | some migrate | Transparent, slow, iridescent comb rows, bioluminescent. Ideal subjects for long observation [L: Haddock 2004; Robison 2004]. |
| **Salps, doliolids, pyrosomes** | cm-long chains to metre-long colonies | variable | many migrate | Come in blooms. Pyrosomes glow brightly when touched. Salp barrels often carry hyperiid amphipods (*Phronima*). |
| **Larvaceans, pteropods, heteropods, chaetognaths, *Tomopteris*** | mm to 10 cm | variable | variable | Delicate mucus houses and feeding webs [L: Hamner & Robison 1992], and transparent swimming snails (*Carinaria*, *Pterotrachea*). Mesobot damaged a giant larvacean's house while following it [search-level lead: Yoerger et al. 2021]. |

Larger visitors follow the migrating layer: tunas, swordfish, dolphins, and whales that feed on
squid at depth. They are rare in the camera's few metres of view but worth recording [E]. The
continuous recorder keeps them.

**What this means for filming.**
- **Gelatinous animals** are the reliable subjects. They do not flee and tolerate long,
  close observation.
- **Fishes, squids and crustaceans** are the shy, mobile subjects. You get them when the vehicle
  is quiet, dark or dim far-red, and slow.

## 3. When and where they are: diel vertical migration

Every evening a large share of the twilight-zone animals swims up toward the surface to feed in
the dark, and every morning back down to hide from visual predators in the dim blue depths. It is
the largest daily animal movement on Earth [L: Hays 2003; Brierley 2014].

- **Kinds of migration** [L: Hays 2003; Pearre 2003]:
  - *Normal*: up at dusk, down at dawn.
  - *Twilight*: up at dusk, sinking around midnight, up again before dawn, then down.
  - *Reverse*: some zooplankton go the other way.
  - *Partial and asynchronous*: individuals migrate when hungry and return when fed. The layer is
    never all-or-nothing.
- **Timing in the Gulf** [V: Ursella et al. 2021; D'Elia et al. 2016]:
  - The shallowest migrants start rising about **1 h before sunset** and start descending about
    **2 h before sunrise**.
  - At dusk part of the layer rises above 100 m but stays below the thermocline.
- **What they follow.** Scattering layers stay at a preferred light level (an isolume, their
  "light comfort zone") and track it as it moves [L: Røstad et al. 2016; Aksnes et al. 2017].
  - They respond to passing cloud shadows within minutes [L: Omand et al. 2021].
  - On bright moonlit nights they stay deeper and ascend later [V for the delay near full moon;
    L: Last et al. 2016; Prihartato et al. 2016].
- **Speed.** Scattering layers move at a few centimetres per second, typically about 1–6 m per
  minute [L: Bianchi & Mislan 2016]. The dusk crossing of 100–200 m therefore takes tens of
  minutes, not seconds [E].
- **By day at 200 m.** The main layer sits at 450–550 m [V: Kaltenberg et al. 2007]. It rose to
  200–300 m near a Mississippi River plume front [V].
  - 200 m is not empty by day. Lanternfishes are caught down from about 200 m, partial migrators
    and non-migrators live there, and gelatinous animals occur at all depths [V: a claim that the
    band is sparse outside the migrations was rejected].
- **At night.** Migrators spread through the upper 200 m, many concentrated in the upper 100 m
  near or just below the thermocline [V, L].
- **Seasons and water masses.** Loop Current water and eddies change vertical distributions and
  species composition [E: reviewers cited recent DEEPEND work; not verified here]. Check where the
  Loop Current is (satellite sea-surface height) before the trip.

## 4. How they are spread horizontally

- **Layers are wide.** Scattering layers stretch for kilometres to hundreds of kilometres [L:
  Klevjer et al. 2016]. For a vehicle that sees a few metres, **depth decides far more than
  horizontal position** [E, from the layer structure].
- **Patches are small.** Within a layer, animals are patchy on scales of metres to hundreds of
  metres:
  - krill swarm;
  - salps and pyrosomes bloom;
  - siphonophores and medusae can be locally common along the same density surface;
  - most lanternfishes and dragonfishes are dispersed singly through the layer rather than in
    tight schools [L: Barham 1966; Kaartvedt et al. 2009].
- **Features concentrate animals** [L: Genin 2004]:
  - fronts and eddy edges;
  - the continental slope, where descending migrators are blocked by the seafloor at dawn;
  - canyons such as DeSoto and Mississippi Canyon.

## 5. What they sense, and how they react to a vehicle

### 5.1 Vision and light colour

- **Blue-tuned eyes.** Most twilight-zone fishes, squids and crustaceans have a single visual
  pigment tuned to blue, peaking near 470–490 nm, matching the downwelling light and most
  bioluminescence. Sensitivity falls steeply above about 550–600 nm [L: Warrant & Locket 2004;
  Turner et al. 2009; Frank & Widder 1999]. So your point about red light is right in
  principle: most of these animals are nearly blind to deep red.
- **Three caveats:**
  1. **Ordinary red LEDs (about 620–660 nm) are not invisible.** They sit on the tail of the
     animals' sensitivity, and at high power animals still see them. In field trials, bright
     red cut acoustic density near the light by 26–83% and dim red by 4–39%, against 72–99% for
     white [V: Geoffroy et al. 2021]. At a seafloor camera, far-red (about 695 nm) kept sablefish
     in view longer than 685 nm red or white [V: Raymond & Widder 2007]. Red light counted 38.9
     sablefish per 10 min against 7.5 under white [V: Widder et al. 2005]. Sablefish are bottom
     fish, so for twilight-zone animals this is an indication, not a measurement.
  2. **A few predators see far-red.** Some dragonfishes (*Malacosteus*, *Aristostomias*,
     *Pachystomias*) make far-red light and see it [L: Widder et al. 1984; Douglas et al. 1998].
  3. **The moment a light comes on is itself a trigger** [V: Raymond & Widder 2007].
- **So: far-red, dim, steady, ramped** (§7.6).

### 5.2 Water movement and pressure waves

- **What they sense.** Fishes feel flow with their lateral line, squids with statocysts and
  hair cells, crustaceans with sensory hairs. Krill and copepods escape explosively from sudden
  flow [L].
- **What this means for the vehicle.** A thruster's wash and a moving hull's bow wave are strong
  cues at metres range [E]. Mesobot was built with large, slowly turning propellers for this
  reason [search-level lead: Yoerger et al. 2021].
- **Slow approaches work better.** 0.25 m/s disturbed animals less than 0.5 m/s [search-level
  lead: Stoner et al. 2008].

### 5.3 Sound

- **Hearing.** Fishes with gas-filled swimbladders hear low-frequency sound well, and vessel noise
  can drive avoidance [L: De Robertis & Handegard 2013].
- **Quieter hearing in the deep.** Many twilight-zone fishes have reduced or fat-filled
  swimbladders and are probably less sound-sensitive [L].
- **Keep thrusters quiet.** Run them at steady low RPM rather than in bursts [E].

### 5.4 The avoidance halo

- **Nets.** Twilight-zone fishes avoid nets so well that trawls greatly underestimate their
  biomass [L: Kaartvedt et al. 2012].
- **Instruments and ROVs** [V: Geoffroy et al. 2021; Benoit-Bird et al. 2023]:
  - an unlit lowered instrument was avoided at 11–24 m;
  - lights doubled that or more;
  - animals kept about 45 m from a lit ROV.

**For a small vehicle: fishes and squids will usually know it is there before the camera sees
them.** They must be met by a vehicle that is quiet, slow, dark or far-red, or motionless.
Gelatinous animals do not flee and are the exception.

## 6. Bioluminescence: the signal in the dark

- **Most animals here make light.** In Monterey Bay, about three quarters of the animals observed
  from the surface to the deep were bioluminescent [L: Martini & Haddock 2017].
- **What they use it for** [L: Haddock et al. 2010; Widder 2010]:
  - **Counter-illumination.** Belly photophores match the light from above so the animal casts
    no silhouette. Lanternfishes, hatchetfishes and many squids do this.
  - **Startle and "burglar alarm" flashes** that attract the attacker's predators.
  - **Decoys.** Shrimps spew luminous clouds; some jellies shed glowing fragments.
  - **Lures.** Dragonfish barbels, siphonophore lures.
  - **Signalling.** Photophore patterns differ by species.
- **The vehicle itself triggers light.** Touch and turbulence make many animals flash. In the dark
  with the lamp off, flashes around the vehicle show where animals are. A camera at high gain
  should register the brighter ones [E: test at night].
- **A night-time cue.** A burst of flashes is the natural thing to turn toward (§7.4).

## 7. The field plan

### 7.1 Timing: dive around the migrations

Timing matters more than anything else in this plan. Example for a night with sunset at 19:30 and
sunrise at 06:30:

| clock | period | depth | vehicle behaviour |
|---|---|---|---|
| 17:30–18:30 | late afternoon | descend to 150–220 m | profile the column once; lamp off (auto) |
| 18:30–21:00 | **dusk ascent** passes through | hold 150–200 m, then follow the layer up | mostly **drift and hover** while the layer rises past (§7.3); short slow legs |
| 21:00–03:30 | night | 50–150 m, near or below the thermocline | slow transects alternating with drifts; dim far-red lamp; turn toward flashes |
| 03:30–06:30 | **pre-dawn descent** passes back down | 100–200 m | as at dusk: hover in the band as the layer sinks past |
| after 06:30 | day | 200 m (optional) | residents and gelatinous animals in dim blue light; lamp off if the camera can see |

- **Moon.** Prefer the nights around new moon. On bright nights the ascent is later and the
  night layer deeper [V, L].
- **Clouds.** Scattering layers react to cloud shadows within minutes, so expect some vertical
  movement on a patchy-cloud evening [L].
- **Season.** Unknown for your site. The first dives' depth-time record of finds shows it (§8).

The planner already follows the dusk and dawn windows (`search.dusk_h`, `dawn_h`) and learns
which depth bands pay off. Set `sunrise_h`/`sunset_h` for the site and date.

### 7.2 Location

- **Deep water.** Offshore beyond the shelf break, over water well deeper than 500 m.
- **Near features.** Prefer the edges of eddies and fronts, and slope areas where migrators
  concentrate [L: Genin 2004]. Look up the Loop Current and eddy positions (satellite sea-surface
  height and temperature) the day before.
- **Stay clear of hazards.** Keep away from oil and gas platforms, their safety zones, and
  shipping lanes.
- **Permits and wildlife.** Check permit needs for your area. Keep clear of marine mammals and
  turtles at the surface [E: regulatory details not checked here].

### 7.3 Search pattern: what to do, and why not a large circle

**Work vertically first.** Depth is the one coordinate the vehicle knows precisely, and it is the
one that decides what you meet (§3, §4). The layer comes to you twice a day.

Horizontal movement is for:
1. sampling water the vehicle has not disturbed yet;
2. finding local patches, such as swarms, blooms and aggregations along a density layer.

| period | pattern | why |
|---|---|---|
| **Dusk and dawn crossings** | **Hover or drift** in the 150–200 m band, lamp off unless it is too dark. Every 5–10 min, make one slow leg of 30–60 m to leave your own disturbance behind. | The migrators cross your depth at a few cm/s [L], so the whole layer passes within an hour. A motionless vehicle is the least frightening [V: avoidance; L: trawl avoidance]. |
| **Night in the upper layer, and day at 200 m** | **Slow straight transects**: about 0.2–0.25 m/s for about 10 min, like MBARI's midwater video transects [search-level lead]. Alternate each with a **3–5 min silent drift**, then turn 90–150° toward water not yet visited. | Transects sample new water. Drifts let shy fishes settle and come back into range. |
| **After a find** | Depends on the animal (§7.4). | Some come in groups, some alone. |

**Why not a large circle:**
- **It revisits water it has already disturbed.** Fishes that moved away are still avoiding that
  water. Bioluminescent plankton that already flashed has less light left to give [E].
- **After the first lap it samples no new water.** A camera sees 2–3 m to each side, so each lap
  retraces the same thin ring. Straight transects with turns toward unvisited water keep reaching
  new water.
- **The centre drifts anyway.** Without a DVL, a circle is only a circle in the water frame, and
  the water carries both the vehicle and the animals.
- **When a circle does make sense:** as a slow loiter around a known aggregation (a krill swarm, a
  salp or pyrosome bloom), with a radius larger than the animals' avoidance distance (tens of
  metres).

The software does this now (docs/SEARCH.md §2): it hovers through the crossings, runs transects
with silent drifts otherwise, and chooses its next move by how the last animal behaved (§7.4).

### 7.4 Curiosity: what to turn toward

"Exploring when noticing something" should follow the biology of what was noticed:

- **Flashes of bioluminescence** (night, lamp off). Turn toward the flash, slow to a creep, bring
  the lamp up slowly to a dim far-red, and look. Flashes are the most reliable sign of animals in
  the dark [E, §6].
- **Silhouettes against the brighter water above** (day, at 200 m). Hunters like hatchetfishes
  look up for them [L]. Tilting the camera 20–30° upward could do the same [E: needs a tilted or
  second camera].
- **A weak, distant detection**, where the model sees "something" below its threshold. Turn toward
  it and approach slowly. Most will be marine snow, so require it to persist for a few frames.
- **After filming an animal, what next depends on the animal:**
  - **Swarming or blooming animals** (krill, salps, pyrosomes, pteropods): stay and search around
    the spot, in widening loops, because more are there.
  - **Solitary fishes and squids:** move on at least tens of metres, beyond the avoidance halo. Any
    neighbours have already moved off.
  - **Gelatinous animals:** they often sit along the same density layer. Hold the same depth
    (±5 m) and continue along the transect.

### 7.5 Approach and filming, group by group

| group | final approach | stand-off | lamp | how long | watch for |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| siphonophores, medusae, ctenophores | ≤ 0.1 m/s, from the side; never point thrusters at them | 0.5–1 m | dim, steady | minutes; they tolerate it | fishing tentacles, lures, feeding, hyperiid riders, flashes |
| salps, pyrosomes, larvaceans | ≤ 0.1 m/s; keep wash away (houses and chains break) | 1 m | dim, steady | minutes | feeding, chains, houses, flashes |
| krill, shrimps | creep; any flow triggers a tail-flip | 1–2 m | far-red (red animals look bright to the camera, but the light is dark to them) | seconds to a minute | swarming, escapes, luminous clouds |
| lanternfishes, hatchetfishes, lightfishes | drift toward rather than thrust | 1–2 m | far-red or off | as long as it holds still | hanging postures, photophores, feeding at night |
| dragonfishes | drift; they are ambushers and often stay | 1 m | far-red | minutes if it stays | lures, posture, strikes |
| squids | wait; let them approach | 1–2 m | far-red | as long as it stays | counter-illumination, colour change, hunting |

Across all groups:
- **Never chase an animal that swims away** (implemented, docs/SEARCH.md §3).
- **Predation, spawning and bioluminescent displays are rare and valuable.** Continuous recording
  keeps them without the vehicle having to predict them.

### 7.6 Red light

- **Colour.** Use far-red LEDs peaking at about 680–700 nm, not ordinary 620–630 nm red (§5.1).
- **Level.** As low as the camera allows, steady, and ramped up slowly. The lamp rule already does
  the ramp and keeps the level steady near animals (docs/SEARCH.md §4).
- **Camera.** The camera must see far-red.
  - A standard camera's infrared-cut filter may block much of 680–700 nm, and the verification
    reviewers flagged this too.
  - Raspberry Pi also sells Camera Module 3 variants without that filter ("NoIR", including a
    wide-angle one; check the current product range).
  - Compare the two in a tank with your far-red LEDs before the first night dive [E].
- **Range is short.** Water absorbs red quickly: about 0.35 per metre at 650 nm and 0.65 per metre
  at 700 nm, even in pure water [L: Pope & Fry 1997]. Far-red therefore lights only the nearest
  1–3 m. That suits close-up filming, but it cannot light a search volume. Search relies on
  ambient light, bioluminescence and a quiet vehicle.
- **The model needs red training images.** Red-lit frames look very different from the white-lit
  images the model trains on; this is a note for the ML stage.

### 7.7 Sensors and what to log

**Your sensors: IMU, compass and depth sensor, no DVL.** They cover what the planner needs:
- **Depth** decides the band.
- **Heading** steers transects and the RELEASE turn.
- **The gyro's turn rate** helps the heading hold.

Without a DVL, horizontal position is the water-relative position dead-reckoned from heading and
commanded speed. That is the right frame here, because the animals drift with the same water.

Two practical points:
1. **Calibrate the compass with the thrusters running.** Motor currents bend the magnetic field,
   so the heading error changes with thrust. A tilt-compensated IMU compass helps.
2. **Add a temperature sensor.** It is cheap and shows the thermocline and the water mass, which
   explain where the layer sits.

Log per dive:
- depth, temperature, heading, time, moon phase, cloud cover;
- the ambient brightness the camera measures, and the lamp level (already logged);
- a boat CTD cast before the dive if possible.

Per encounter, label the animal group afterwards from the video (§2 table). The dive report
(`python -m talosaur.onboard.dive_report`) turns the logs into finds per depth band per hour.

### 7.8 Operating at 200 m

- **Drift and recovery.** At an average 0.5 m/s drift (plausible near the Loop Current), a 10 h
  night moves the vehicle 18 km [E].
  - Fix the position by GPS at the surface before and after the dive.
  - Carry a surface beacon: GPS with radio or satellite, plus a strobe.
  - Plan recovery downstream.
- **Hardware.**
  - **Depth rating.** Test to 1.5× the working depth.
  - **Camera window.** It must stay optically flat under pressure.
- **Battery endurance** sets the schedule: prioritise dusk and pre-dawn if the night cannot be
  covered.
- **Night hazards.**
  - **Cookiecutter sharks** bite neat round chunks out of large animals in warm oceanic waters
    at night [L: Jones 1971]. They have reportedly damaged submarine sonar domes too [not
    checked]. Whether they occur at your site was not checked either.
  - Soft or protruding parts may attract other bites [E].

### 7.9 Lakes first: the same behaviour at a smaller scale

- **Deep lakes migrate too.** Opossum shrimps (*Mysis*), phantom midge larvae (*Chaoborus*) and
  *Daphnia* rise from depth at dusk and sink at dawn. Planktivorous fishes such as cisco, kokanee
  and smelt follow them, for the same predator-avoidance reasons [L: Lampert 1989].
- **Rehearse the plan at dusk in a lake at least 30–50 m deep:**
  - profile, then hold the band;
  - drift periods;
  - lamp behaviour in the dark;
  - approach without chasing.
- **Measure:**
  - what the camera sees by moonlight and with far-red;
  - how close fish let the vehicle come, quiet versus thrusting.

## 9. What this asks of the software

These change the vehicle's behaviour, not the ML model. Items 1–3 are done (docs/SEARCH.md §2).

1. **Hover and drift periods. Done.**
   - Through the dusk and dawn crossings, the vehicle hovers (holds depth and heading, no forward
     thrust) with a short slow leg every 7 min.
   - Otherwise it runs 10-min transects, each followed by a 4-min silent drift.
2. **Next move by how the animal behaved. Done**, without an ML label. The encounter is
   classified from what the vehicle saw:
   - **swarm** (several animals in view): widening loops around the spot;
   - **mobile** (it swam away or was lost): move on 40 m, beyond the avoidance halo;
   - **drifter** (it stayed): hold its depth and carry on along the layer.

   A group label from the model (fish, squid, jelly, crustacean) would sharpen this later.
3. **Curiosity. Done.**
   - The vehicle turns toward and creeps up to bioluminescent flashes (brief local brightenings in
     blue-green) and to weak detections that persist.
   - The lamp is left as it is, since a light coming on scares animals.
   - Whether the Camera Module 3 registers real flashes at depth is untested (§8).
4. **Upward-looking silhouettes by day.** A camera tilt or a second camera.
5. **Temperature input.** Log it and show the thermocline in the dive report.
